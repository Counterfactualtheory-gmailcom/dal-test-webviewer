Beyond Accuracy: Governance-First AI for Regulated Institutions

Why authorization comes before accuracy, the governance standard any AI system serving a regulated institution should meet, and how COMPAiSS meets it.

For most of the short history of generative AI, one question has dominated evaluation: was the answer correct? For regulated institutions, that question is necessary but not sufficient. A university, hospital, or public agency must also know whether the system was permitted to answer, and whether every institution-specific statement came from sources the institution has authorized. An answer can be fluent, confident, and even factually correct, and still carry no institutional authority at all.

COMPAiSS is built on that distinction. Its execution-gated architecture determines whether institution-specific inference is authorized before any answer is generated. If the institution's approved sources cannot support an answer, the model does not run. Its evaluation methodology measures what regulated institutions actually require: preservation of institutional authority, source authority, and correct authorization decisions, alongside accuracy.

This reorders how institutional AI should be judged. Accuracy remains essential, but it does not outrank authorization. The governance standard described on this page is the standard any AI system proposed for regulated institutional use should be required to meet. It is not a standard designed around COMPAiSS. It follows from what regulated institutions are accountable for.

An unauthorized truth is as unacceptable to a regulated institution as a hallucinated one. The question is not only whether an answer is correct, but whether the system had the authority to give it.

Why accuracy is not enough

COMPAiSS is a response to a problem that exists independently of it, and the governance literature, not COMPAiSS, defines that problem. The starting point is an empirical correction. Regulated institutions have not rejected AI, and it would be inaccurate to claim they have. What the evidence shows is narrower and more useful: adoption is real but cautious, uneven, concentrated in lower-consequence uses, and strongly mediated by governance readiness rather than by model capability alone.

The magnitudes are instructive. A national analysis of American Hospital Association data found roughly 18.7 percent of United States hospitals had adopted at least one form of AI, with only 3.82 percent classified as high adopters, and adoption concentrated in workflow, demand prediction, and staffing rather than high-consequence clinical decisions (Bin et al., 2024). More recent government and survey data show adoption continuing to rise while remaining uneven, lower among small, rural, independent, and critical-access hospitals, and still facing unresolved questions of real-world performance and demonstrated value (ONC, 2026). Generative AI is more uneven still: ambient clinical documentation is the most broadly deployed use, while chart summarization, patient engagement, and diagnostic applications remain largely at pilot stage, and immature tooling is repeatedly named among the leading barriers (Poon et al., 2025).

Two conclusions follow, and both bear directly on how any institutional AI system should be judged. First, the barriers are not primarily about model accuracy. A systematic review of the barriers to AI implementation grouped them as ethical, technological, liability and regulatory, workforce, social, and patient-safety, with technological and ethical concerns most frequent and accuracy only one component among many (Ahmed et al., 2023). The implementation literature adds privacy, interoperability, workflow integration, professional trust, explainability, accountability, liability, drift, and post-deployment monitoring (Hassan et al., 2024). Second, deployment and governance are not the same thing. A Canadian hospital case study found an organization already running five vendor AI products yet lacking a formal governance structure, with practices described as effectively ad hoc until a centralized committee and lifecycle oversight were built (Kim et al., 2025). International guidance converges on the same requirements: the World Health Organization's guidance on the ethics and governance of AI for health and the NIST AI Risk Management Framework both foreground provenance, accountability, validation, monitoring, and human oversight as conditions for responsible deployment (WHO, 2021; NIST, 2023).

This literature establishes, independently of COMPAiSS, that regulated institutions face governance requirements that accuracy benchmarks were never designed to measure: provenance, authority, accountability, and auditability. The logic runs in one direction. Institutions define the governance requirements. Those requirements define the evaluation criteria. Any AI system seeking institutional deployment must then be measured against those criteria. A system that performs well on accuracy benchmarks but cannot demonstrate governance has not yet met the standard regulated institutions require.

Authorization before accuracy

Regulated institutions care not only whether an answer is wrong, but how it is wrong and what follows from it. A governance-grade evaluation therefore asks its questions in order:

OrderQuestionWhy it comes first
1. AuthorizationWas the system permitted to produce an institution-specific answer at all?If authorized evidence was insufficient, any institutional answer, correct or not, is an answer the institution never sanctioned.
2. Source authorityDid every institution-specific claim come from institution-authorized sources?Provenance is what makes an answer the institution's answer, and what makes it auditable.
3. AccuracyWas the answer correct, complete, and useful?Essential, and meaningful for an institution only once authorization and source authority are established.

The COMPAiSS methodology classifies outcomes accordingly. A Type A outcome preserves institutional authority: the system declines to answer rather than go beyond its authorized sources, and the missing content is flagged for the institution. A Type C outcome is a traceable pipeline issue that leaves governance intact. A Type B outcome is the one institutions most need to prevent: institution-specific content, presented in the institution's name, without authorizing evidence, such as a treatment, an eligibility rule, a deadline, or a legal obligation a user may act on (Ahmed et al., 2023; WHO, 2021; NIST, 2023).

Accuracy-only benchmarks cannot draw these distinctions. They score a safe, correct refusal and an authoritative-sounding invented policy on the same scale. A governance standard separates them, because the consequences for an institution are entirely different.

The governance standard

Any AI system proposed for regulated institutional deployment should be measured on the following criteria, in addition to conventional accuracy:

  • Governance Preservation Rate (GPR): the share of answers with no governance violation.
  • Authorized Coverage Rate (ACR): the share of answerable, authorized questions actually answered from authorized evidence. Paired with GPR, it ensures strong governance is never achieved simply by declining difficult questions.
  • Constitutional Fidelity: whether answers respect the institution's defined scope and rules.
  • Source Authority Fidelity: whether institution-specific claims come from authorized sources.
  • Authorization Accuracy: whether the system correctly decides when inference is and is not permitted.

The burden of proof follows from the standard. Generation-first systems, including general-purpose assistants and enterprise retrieval systems, are designed to produce an answer by default and manage risk afterward. When they are proposed for regulated institutional use, it is for them to demonstrate that they preserve institutional authority, confine institution-specific claims to authorized sources, and decide correctly when not to answer. Accuracy on general benchmarks does not answer those questions.

An institution evaluating any AI system can ask its vendor five direct questions:

  1. Can the system decline to generate when your authorized sources do not support an answer, and does it?
  2. Can every institution-specific claim be traced to a source your institution approved?
  3. Is the boundary of authorized sources controlled by your institution, with an auditable change history?
  4. Are governance outcomes reported separately from accuracy, with safe refusals distinguished from unauthorized answers?
  5. Can these properties be demonstrated on your own questions, before deployment?

COMPAiSS answers yes to each, by architecture.

How COMPAiSS meets the standard

COMPAiSS's governance properties are architectural, not configurable settings. They operate at three levels.

If authorization fails, the institutional reasoning model does not run. The rule is enforced by the architecture rather than the model, so it cannot be prompted, reframed, or manipulated away.
Constrained within scopeHow authorized sources are read and applied in answersThe model receives only pre-parsed, institution-authorized content in a tightly bounded context, under instruction-based constraints, and every cited link is validated against the authorized source set after generation.

Missing authorized content surfaces as a flagged, correctable gap in coverage, never as an invented answer.

The institution remains the authority on its own content, as it should. COMPAiSS ensures the AI never speaks for the institution beyond that content, and that every answer is traceable to the authorized source it came from.

Common misconceptions

MisconceptionThe reality
"It is just retrieval-augmented generation with new terminology"Retrieval decides what evidence to give a model that will answer regardless. COMPAiSS decides whether the authorized evidence permits institution-specific inference at all. That is a different governance mechanism, not a relabelling.
"It is merely a source whitelist"The approved source list is one component. The architecture connects authorization, retrieval and parsing, an evidential sufficiency threshold, execution gating, governed refusal, and evidence preservation.
"A more accurate model would make it unnecessary"More capable models draw on more training data, which increases the opportunity for other institutions', jurisdictions', and years' information to enter an answer, and makes such answers more convincing. Governance becomes more important as models improve, not less.
"Declining to answer is a failure"For a regulated institution, declining when authorized sources are insufficient is the correct outcome. It preserves institutional authority and shows the institution exactly where its content can be extended. The Authorized Coverage Rate ensures this is never rewarded at the expense of answering what can be answered.
"The approved source list guarantees the institution's content is correct"COMPAiSS guarantees provenance: every answer comes from what the institution authorized. The institution remains the authority on its own content, with audits and dashboards to keep it current.
"It claims automatic legal compliance"COMPAiSS provides a documented, auditable governance architecture that institutions map to their own legal and regulatory obligations.

Questions institutions ask

How has COMPAiSS been evaluated, and can we verify the results ourselves?

COMPAiSS has been evaluated across four institutional deployments in higher education and healthcare under its published governance framework, with an aggregate Governance Preservation Rate of 98.7 percent across 159 evaluated answers (see The evidence). The questions, scoring criteria, and outcome definitions are published, so any institution can apply the same framework to its own questions, to COMPAiSS and to any other system it is considering.

How does COMPAiSS compare with enterprise retrieval (RAG) systems?

They are built to answer different questions. Retrieval systems improve what evidence reaches a model that will generate regardless. COMPAiSS governs whether institution-specific generation is authorized at all. For regulated use, the relevant comparison is the governance standard, and institutions should ask any retrieval-based vendor to demonstrate the same governance properties (see The governance standard).

How does COMPAiSS keep answers faithful to their sources?

The model receives only pre-parsed, institution-authorized content in a tightly bounded context, is instructed to answer from that content alone, and has every cited link validated against the authorized source set. Every answer is traceable to the specific authorized source it came from, so its fidelity can be checked directly.

What happens when an approved source is outdated or missing?

COMPAiSS reflects what the institution has authorized; it does not reinterpret or supplement it. A purpose-built audit server checks every approved source for broken links, redirects, and moved pages, and every change to the source list is logged and attributed. When authorized content does not cover a question, COMPAiSS declines and flags the gap in coverage, so the institution sees exactly where to extend its content (see How COMPAiSS meets the standard).

What exactly does the execution gate guarantee?

Institution-specific inference does not occur unless authorization conditions are met. The rule is a property of the architecture rather than the model, so it cannot be prompted, reframed, or manipulated away. Within authorized scope, answers are further constrained by bounded context, instruction constraints, and link validation.

How does COMPAiSS handle security threats?

Because the gate runs before the model, out-of-scope and manipulative requests are rejected before any inference occurs, on the first attempt and every subsequent one. Inputs are length-capped and filtered, requests are rate-limited, and the platform is protected by Cloudflare. Confining evidence to an audited set of approved sources is itself a security control.

What drives COMPAiSS's lower operating costs?

Queries that fail authorization never reach the model, so they consume little or no inference cost, and the architecture requires no vector databases, embedding pipelines, or index management. COMPAiSS's published cost analysis models the resulting savings against typical enterprise retrieval deployments.

How does COMPAiSS scale?

Governance scales by delegation: each department that already owns institutional content manages its portion of the approved source list through the same dashboard and audit cycle. Source validation is a lightweight status check rather than a document re-read, so even a list of roughly 10,000 sources audits in minutes.

Is the architecture new?

COMPAiSS's contribution is a specific combination: institution-authorized evidential sufficiency as a precondition for institution-specific inference, enforced by an execution gate before generation. That combination is the subject of patent applications in Canada and the United States.

Couldn't a system score well on governance simply by refusing difficult questions?

No. The standard pairs Governance Preservation Rate with Authorized Coverage Rate, which measures how many answerable, authorized questions the system actually answered from authorized evidence. Strong governance counts only alongside strong coverage.

Applying the standard

The governance standard is published so that it can be used. Institutions can apply it in three ways:

  • In procurement: require any AI vendor to report governance outcomes, not only accuracy, using the criteria above.
  • In evaluation: run the same questions through every candidate system, including COMPAiSS, and score each on authorization, source authority, and accuracy, in that order.
  • In operation: use compliance receipts and governance reports to keep a continuing, auditable record of how institutional authority is preserved.

COMPAiSS continues to publish its evaluations under the same framework, and invites institutions and independent evaluators to apply it, to COMPAiSS and to any system proposed for regulated institutional use.

Conclusion

Regulated institutions have been asked to judge AI by a single measure, accuracy, that was never designed for them. The institutional literature shows that what they actually require is authority, provenance, accountability, and auditability. COMPAiSS was built to meet those requirements by architecture, and its methodology measures them directly.

Three propositions follow. First, governance and institutional readiness are central to regulated AI deployment, as the external literature establishes independently of COMPAiSS. Second, authorization must come before accuracy: an institutional answer is acceptable only if the system was permitted to give it and every institutional claim comes from an authorized source. Third, any AI system proposed for regulated institutional use should be held to that standard. COMPAiSS meets it, with an aggregate Governance Preservation Rate of 98.7 percent across four institutional deployments.

Accuracy tells an institution whether an answer is correct. Authorization tells it whether the answer is theirs. Regulated institutions need both, in that order.
