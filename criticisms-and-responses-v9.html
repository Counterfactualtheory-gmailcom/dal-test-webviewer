Beyond Accuracy: Governance-First AI for Regulated Institutions

What the governance literature establishes about the institutional adoption problem, how COMPAiSS responds to it, what independent reviews of the architecture found, and the evidence supporting it.

COMPAiSS is not, in the first instance, an attempt to answer questions more accurately than retrieval-augmented generation or a frontier model. It addresses one of the principal governance barriers that has slowed the adoption of generative AI in regulated institutions: hospitals, universities, governments, and public agencies. A growing governance literature independently identifies institutional governance, regulatory uncertainty, accountability, auditability, privacy, and organizational readiness as major determinants of whether these institutions deploy AI. Where institutions have held back, it has generally not been because general systems cannot produce useful answers. It has been because institutional deployment requires something general capability does not supply: assurance that the system speaks only with the authority the institution has granted, with provenance, accountability, and an auditable record.

That is the problem the architecture was built for, and what its evaluation methodology was built to measure. Conventional benchmarks ask whether an answer was correct, complete, or preferred. The COMPAiSS methodology asks the questions regulated institutions ask before trusting institution-specific reasoning: whether institutional authority was preserved, whether every institution-specific claim came from authorized evidence, whether inference occurred only when authorized evidence was sufficient, whether unauthorized institutional reasoning was prevented, and whether the result is auditable. Both sets of questions are legitimate. They measure different things.

Three adversarial reviews of the public COMPAiSS corpus, conducted separately by three frontier AI models, converged on a clear finding: the core architectural idea holds. None sustained the charge that COMPAiSS is ordinary retrieval with new vocabulary. Each concluded that making sufficient institution-authorized evidence a precondition for institution-specific inference is a real and materially different governance approach.

The evidence supporting the architecture is layered. It begins with an external governance literature, independent of COMPAiSS, documenting that regulated deployment is mediated by governance readiness rather than model accuracy alone. It continues through the governance-first architectural theory, the comparative reasoning for why authorization before inference differs from inference-first design, and a body of comparative empirical evidence: cross-model measurements of hallucination and error rates, controlled gated-versus-ungated comparisons, a public-agency comparison, adversarial stress tests, and four institutional deployment evaluations. Independent external validation is the next stage of that program, and institutions are invited to take part in it.

The core contribution is the authorization-first governance architecture itself: institutional AI that governs whether institution-specific inference may occur at all, rather than trying to improve the probability of a correct answer after generation has begun.

The institutional problem the literature documents

COMPAiSS is a response to a problem that exists independently of it, and the governance literature, not COMPAiSS, defines that problem. The starting point is an empirical correction. Regulated institutions have not rejected AI, and it would be inaccurate to claim they have. What the evidence shows is narrower and more useful: adoption is real but cautious, uneven, concentrated in lower-consequence uses, and strongly mediated by governance readiness rather than by model capability alone.

The magnitudes are instructive. A national analysis of American Hospital Association data found roughly 18.7 percent of United States hospitals had adopted at least one form of AI, with only 3.82 percent classified as high adopters, and adoption concentrated in workflow, demand prediction, and staffing rather than high-consequence clinical decisions (Bin et al., 2024). More recent government and survey data show adoption continuing to rise while remaining uneven, lower among small, rural, independent, and critical-access hospitals, and still facing unresolved questions of real-world performance and demonstrated value (ONC, 2026). Generative AI is more uneven still: ambient clinical documentation is the most broadly deployed use, while chart summarization, patient engagement, and diagnostic applications remain largely at pilot stage, and immature tooling is repeatedly named among the leading barriers (Poon et al., 2025).

Two conclusions follow, and both bear directly on how any institutional AI system should be judged. First, the barriers are not primarily about model accuracy. A systematic review of the barriers to AI implementation grouped them as ethical, technological, liability and regulatory, workforce, social, and patient-safety, with technological and ethical concerns most frequent and accuracy only one component among many (Ahmed et al., 2023). The implementation literature adds privacy, interoperability, workflow integration, professional trust, explainability, accountability, liability, drift, and post-deployment monitoring (Hassan et al., 2024). Second, deployment and governance are not the same thing. A Canadian hospital case study found an organization already running five vendor AI products yet lacking a formal governance structure, with practices described as effectively ad hoc until a centralized committee and lifecycle oversight were built (Kim et al., 2025). International guidance converges on the same requirements: the World Health Organization's guidance on the ethics and governance of AI for health and the NIST AI Risk Management Framework both foreground provenance, accountability, validation, monitoring, and human oversight as conditions for responsible deployment (WHO, 2021; NIST, 2023).

This literature does the most important work in the paper, and it does it before COMPAiSS is mentioned. It establishes that regulated institutions face governance requirements that conventional accuracy benchmarks were never designed to measure but that institutions routinely require before trusting institution-specific reasoning. A governance-oriented evaluation construct is therefore legitimate independently of COMPAiSS. The chain runs in one direction: institutions define the governance requirements; those requirements imply evaluation criteria that accuracy benchmarks do not capture; a governance-oriented methodology follows. The need for a governance-oriented evaluation construct is independently motivated by the institutional literature. COMPAiSS proposes one operationalization of that construct, an architecture and a rubric, whose reliability, validity, completeness, and comparative usefulness remain subject to independent evaluation. The literature supports the importance of measuring governance; it does not certify the COMPAiSS methodology as the correct, complete, or uniquely appropriate way to measure it. Healthcare supplies the best-documented version of this problem; the problem is not healthcare-only, but healthcare illustrates, in unusual empirical detail, how regulated institutional deployment differs from ordinary consumer or enterprise software adoption.

Consequence asymmetry, and why the failure taxonomy matters

The same literature explains why COMPAiSS's failure taxonomy is not an internal preference but a reflection of how institutions actually bear risk. Regulated institutions care not only whether an answer is wrong, but how it is wrong and what follows from it. The three classes map onto that asymmetry. A Type A failure produces poor utility while preserving institutional authority: the system declines or under-answers, which is a usability cost, not a governance breach. A Type C failure is a traceable pipeline defect that leaves governance intact, where authorized evidence was discovered but not delivered. A Type B failure is the one institutions most need to prevent: unauthorized institution-specific content that a user may act upon, a claim about a treatment, an eligibility rule, a deadline, a legal obligation, or a benefit, made in the institution's name without authorizing evidence. The adoption literature's emphasis on safety, accountability, liability, and institutional responsibility is, in effect, an emphasis on the Type B region (Ahmed et al., 2023; WHO, 2021; NIST, 2023).

This also reframes the hallucination question. The claim is not that the architecture does not hallucinate. It is that authorization-first execution is designed to structurally preclude certain failure classes that carry disproportionate institutional consequences, to reduce the probability of others, to leave residual model errors possible after inference begins, and to do nothing about source-quality failures that no architecture solves automatically. Separating those four is more honest, and more useful to a regulated institution weighing liability and patient or user safety, than any blanket claim about hallucination.

Common misconceptions, and what the reviews found

Several objections commonly raised about governance-first AI were examined by the reviewers against the full corpus and set aside.

MisconceptionWhat the reviewers found
"It is just ordinary RAG with new terminology"RAG and COMPAiSS share components, but the governing question differs: RAG asks what evidence to give the model; COMPAiSS asks whether the authorized evidence permits institutional inference at all. Reviewers found this a meaningful distinction, not a relabeling.
"It is merely source whitelisting"Whitelisting is one component. The architecture connects authorization, retrieval and parsing, an evidential threshold, execution gating, governed refusal, and evidence preservation. Reducing it to the list misses the mechanism.
"There is no coherent theory; it is marketing"All three reviewers found a consistent architectural theory argued throughout the corpus. Conceptual coherence was confirmed.
"It claims to eliminate every hallucination"The current framework rejects that reading, splitting failures into knowledge, governance, and execution classes and claiming structural prevention only for defined classes.
"The greenlist guarantees the institution's information is correct"Not a claim COMPAiSS makes. Provenance is guaranteed under the architecture's conditions; correctness of an authorized source is not. A stale authorized policy remains stale.
"It claims automatic legal compliance"The regulatory material treats its scoring as alignment analysis, states there is no official rubric, and calls for independent legal review. The corpus itself answers this directly.
"It has no empirical evidence"False. The evidence base extends well beyond the architecture: 159 evaluated answers across four deployments, plus comparative material, cross-model hallucination measurements, gated-versus-ungated comparisons, a public-agency comparison, and a residual-hallucination categorization. Independent replication is the next stage of that evidence program.

The evidence to date

The methodology classifies every failure by type rather than collapsing results into a single accuracy score. Type A is a coverage limit where the system declines rather than invents and governance is preserved. Type B is a governance violation. Type C is an architectural failure where authorized evidence was found but did not reach inference.

The most important finding sits beneath the numbers. Neither governance violation involved the system inventing an institutional position from general model knowledge. Both occurred at the boundary between authorized material and the claim drawn from it, where an answer extended an authorized source slightly beyond what it established. Both were traceable to a specific source and correctable at that source. Every Type A result also did useful work: it pinpointed authorized content not yet on the source list, such as a missing page, without the system substituting general knowledge.

The deployment evaluations are complemented by further lines of evidence:

  • Governance stress tests. Two adversarial evaluations tested the architecture under institutional failure conditions, including conflicting policies, unclear authority, and an imminent student-safety risk. No generative leakage and no fabricated authority were observed. Where authority could not be established from authorized sources, the system escalated to documented decision-makers. (Stress test 1, stress test 2)
  • Cross-system benchmarking. Across two evaluations of the same production question, scored on twelve weighted institutional governance criteria, COMPAiSS was the only one of four systems never to incur a hallucination or safety penalty. (Demos and benchmarking)
  • Gated versus ungated comparisons. Live demonstrations across active pilots pair each governed answer with the output of the same underlying model running without the execution gate, showing directly what the gate prevents. (Live demonstrations)

The evaluation framework is published so that institutions can apply it to their own questions and compare results.

What the architecture guarantees

Regulated institutions are best served by a precise statement of what an architecture does, because precision is what makes a guarantee auditable. COMPAiSS's guarantees operate at three levels.

LevelWhat it coversHow
Structurally preventedInstitution-specific generation when authorized evidence is insufficient; answers outside the institution's defined scope; reliance on general model knowledge in place of institutional sourcesThe pre-inference execution gate. If authorization fails, the primary institutional reasoning model does not run. The rule is enforced by the architecture, not by the model, so it does not depend on the model behaving well.
Materially reducedGeneration-quality risks within authorized scope, such as misreading or over-extending an authorized sourceThe model receives only pre-parsed, institution-authorized content in a tightly bounded context, under instruction-based constraints, with post-generation validation that every cited link belongs to the authorized source set.
Made visible and correctableThe currency and completeness of the institution's own authorized contentGreenlist audits, a governance dashboard with attributed change history, and compliance receipts. Missing authorized content surfaces as a missing answer flagged for correction, not as an incorrect answer delivered to a user.

This division is deliberate. The accuracy of an institution's official content is, and should remain, the institution's responsibility; no AI system can make an outdated policy current. What COMPAiSS contributes is that it will not speak for the institution beyond that content, and that every answer can be traced to the specific authorized source it came from.

The same test for every architecture

The COMPAiSS methodology defines governance as the outcome of interest, and it applies equally to any system proposed for regulated deployment. Its measures are:

  • Governance Preservation Rate (GPR): the share of answers with no governance violation.
  • Authorized Coverage Rate (ACR): the share of answerable, authorized questions actually answered from authorized evidence. It is paired with GPR so that strong governance can never be achieved simply by refusing difficult questions.
  • Constitutional Fidelity: whether answers respect the institution's defined scope and rules.
  • Source Authority Fidelity: whether institution-specific claims come from authorized sources.
  • Authorization Accuracy: whether the system correctly decides when inference is and is not permitted.

Once success is defined as preservation of institutional authority, the question is no longer only whether COMPAiSS can match enterprise retrieval systems on accuracy. It is whether inference-first architectures, including the most sophisticated permission-aware retrieval systems, can meet the same governance standard when evaluated against it. COMPAiSS invites exactly that comparison. The methodology binds COMPAiSS as much as any comparator, and it is published so that institutions and independent evaluators can apply it.

Questions institutions ask

These are the questions procurement officers, IT security teams, and governance committees most often raise when evaluating COMPAiSS, with direct answers.

How has COMPAiSS been evaluated, and can we verify the results ourselves?

COMPAiSS has been evaluated across four institutional deployments in higher education and healthcare under a shared, published governance methodology, with an aggregate Governance Preservation Rate of 98.7 percent across 159 evaluated answers (see The evidence to date). The questions, scoring criteria, and failure definitions are published, so any institution can apply the same framework to its own questions and compare results. Independent multi-evaluator evaluation is the next milestone in the research program, and pilot institutions are invited to take part.

How does COMPAiSS compare with enterprise retrieval (RAG) systems?

They answer different questions. RAG decides which evidence to give a model that will generate regardless. COMPAiSS decides whether the authorized evidence permits institution-specific inference at all. The fair comparison is therefore on governance criteria rather than generic accuracy, and COMPAiSS invites that comparison against the strongest permission-aware enterprise systems (see The same test for every architecture). A governance head-to-head evaluation under identical conditions is part of the research program.

How does COMPAiSS keep answers faithful to their sources?

The model receives only pre-parsed, institution-authorized content in a tightly bounded context, is instructed to answer from that content alone, and has every cited link validated against the authorized source set after generation. Across the four deployment evaluations, no governance violation involved the system inventing an institutional position from general knowledge; both recorded violations were slight extensions of an authorized source, each traceable to that source and correctable there. A claim-level fidelity benchmark, checking every institutional claim against its cited evidence, is part of the research program.

What happens when an approved source is outdated or missing?

COMPAiSS reflects what the institution has authorized; it does not reinterpret or supplement it. A purpose-built audit server checks every approved source for broken links, redirects, and moved pages, and every change to the source list is logged and attributed. When authorized content does not cover a question, the result is a missing answer flagged for correction rather than an incorrect answer delivered to a user, so the institution can see exactly where its content needs attention (see What the architecture guarantees).

What exactly does the execution gate guarantee?

The gate enforces one rule deterministically: institution-specific inference does not occur unless authorization conditions are met. That rule is a property of the architecture, not of the model, and it cannot be prompted, reframed, or manipulated away. Within authorized scope, the AI components that interpret questions and compose answers remain probabilistic, which is why the architecture adds bounded context, instruction constraints, and post-generation link validation. Automated verification of the gate's rule through execution traces is part of the research program.

How does COMPAiSS handle security threats, including to approved sources?

Because the gate runs before the model, out-of-scope and manipulative requests are rejected before any inference occurs. Inputs are length-capped and filtered, requests are rate-limited, and the platform is protected by Cloudflare. Restricting evidence to an approved source set is itself a security control, and approved sources are audited continuously. A formal threat model covering source compromise, indirect injection, and redirects, together with a third-party penetration test, is part of the research program.

What drives COMPAiSS's lower operating costs?

Queries that fail authorization never reach the model, so they consume little or no inference cost, and the architecture requires no vector databases, embedding pipelines, or index management. COMPAiSS's published cost analysis models the resulting savings against typical enterprise RAG deployments. Measured total-cost-of-ownership comparisons from live deployments are part of the research program.

How does COMPAiSS scale?

Governance scales by delegation: each department that already owns institutional content manages its portion of the approved source list through the same dashboard and audit cycle. Source validation is a lightweight status check rather than a document re-read, so even a list of roughly 10,000 sources audits in minutes. Load, latency, and availability reporting under measured production load is part of the research program.

Is the architecture new?

COMPAiSS builds on a broader movement toward placing controls upstream of generation, and names related work openly. Its contribution is a specific combination: institution-authorized evidential sufficiency as a precondition for institution-specific inference, enforced by an execution gate. That combination is the subject of patent applications in Canada and the United States, and peer-reviewed publication is part of the research program.

Couldn't a system score well on governance simply by refusing difficult questions?

No, and the methodology is designed to prevent it. Governance Preservation Rate is paired with an Authorized Coverage Rate, which measures how many answerable, authorized questions the system actually answered from authorized evidence. Strong governance results count only alongside strong coverage.

Conclusion

COMPAiSS is best understood not as a bid to answer questions more accurately than retrieval-augmented generation or a frontier model, but as a response to a governance barrier that independent research identifies as a central constraint on the adoption of generative AI in regulated institutions. Its methodology measures preservation of institutional authority rather than answer accuracy. The reviews confirmed the central proposition: that institutional AI should govern the authority to produce institution-specific inference from a defined body of authorized evidence, rather than merely improve the odds of a correct answer after a question has been asked.

Three propositions follow. First, that governance and institutional readiness are central to regulated AI deployment is established by the external literature, independently of COMPAiSS. Second, COMPAiSS has been implemented across multiple regulated institutional settings, evaluated under a shared methodology that reported its results transparently, and supported by converging architectural, comparative, and empirical evidence. Third, the question now facing the field is which architectures best satisfy the governance requirements regulated institutions actually face, when every system is evaluated against the same governance criteria.

The internal evidence is strong enough to warrant rigorous independent confirmation, and that is the stage COMPAiSS has reached. Institutions evaluating AI for regulated environments can apply the same governance test to any system they are considering, and COMPAiSS welcomes that comparison.
